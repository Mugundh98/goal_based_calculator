# Retirement planning

## Two phases

Retirement planning has an accumulation phase, from today until retirement, when savings and SIPs build a corpus, and a withdrawal phase, from retirement until the end of the plan, when the corpus pays the household's expenses. Retirement can last 25 years or more, so the plan must cope with inflation for a long time after the salary stops.

## How big a corpus

GoalWise grows today's monthly expenses by inflation to the retirement year, then assumes they keep rising with inflation every year after retirement. The corpus needed is the present value, at retirement, of that rising stream of withdrawals, discounted at the return the corpus earns after retirement (8% a year by default). With exactly that corpus, the last withdrawal at the planned age empties it. The default plan runs until age 85; planning for a longer life is safer.

## Withdrawal rate and the 4% rule

The withdrawal rate is the first year's withdrawal as a share of the corpus. William Bengen's 1994 study of US markets found that withdrawing 4% of the corpus in the first year and raising it with inflation survived every 30-year period he tested; this became the "4% rule". Indian inflation is higher, so a safe rate in India is often taken to be lower. GoalWise shows the user's own withdrawal rate next to the 4% rule.

## Longevity and sequence risk

The two biggest risks are living longer than planned and a market fall early in retirement, when the corpus is largest and withdrawals force selling at low prices. GoalWise's lifetime Monte Carlo simulation shows the chance that the money lasts to the planned age, the age by which it runs out in bad cases, and the corpus or SIP needed for a 50%, 75% or 90% chance.

## Where retirement money comes from

Employees' Provident Fund (EPF), Public Provident Fund (PPF), the National Pension System (NPS) and mutual funds all count towards the retirement corpus; their current values can be entered as savings already set aside.
